## Evidence references

Captured directly from the local regtest node. Alice was funded with three
separate 0.4 BTC transactions, confirmed into a single block (all three UTXOs
show `confirmations: 1` in `listunspent`). A subsequent 1 BTC payment from
Alice's wallet an amount no single 0.4 BTC UTXO can cover forced Bitcoin
Core's coin selection to combine all three funding UTXOs as inputs, visible
directly in the spend's `vin` array (3 entries, matching all three funding
txids exactly).

Manual audit:
- Total input: 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 BTC
- Output `n=1` (1.00000000 BTC) -> `bcrt1qh2yl2wg8m60pc44xsf2uaqhkvglwmrgmw9qlvz`, the
  `receiver` wallet's address this is the **payment**.
- Output `n=0` (0.19997240 BTC) → `bcrt1qu675znwdpwgwg5gyfhhf4f2zq6r522m45lhcvq`, a
  fresh address auto-generated by Alice's wallet, not seen anywhere before
  this is the **change**.
- Fee = 1.2 - (0.19997240 + 1.00000000) = **0.0000276 BTC**

## Explanation (co-authored by Claude)

Bitcoin Core's wallet does not require manually picking which UTXOs to spend
its coin selection algorithm automatically chooses whichever combination
of a wallet's available UTXOs is needed to satisfy a requested payment
amount. Here, Alice held three separate 0.4 BTC UTXOs (1.2 BTC total combined),
and requested a 1 BTC payment an amount no single UTXO could cover on its
own, since 0.4 < 1. The wallet had no choice but to combine multiple inputs,
and in this case it used all three, since covering 1 BTC requires more value
than any two of the 0.4 BTC UTXOs together (0.8 BTC) can provide.

Whenever the sum of selected inputs exceeds the requested payment amount, the
excess is not simply discarded or handed to the miner the wallet
automatically creates a change output, returning the leftover value to a
fresh address it controls. This is exactly what happened here: after covering
the 1 BTC payment and a small fee, the remaining 0.19997240 BTC was sent back
to Alice via a newly generated change address, rather than to the same
address that originally received the three funding transactions.

That last detail carries a real privacy implication. Because this single
transaction combines three previously separate UTXOs into one spend, anyone
observing the blockchain can now reasonably infer that all three original
funding transactions belong to the same owner only someone holding all
three private keys could have authorized spending them together in one
transaction. This pattern is known as "UTXO linking" or "common-input-ownership
heuristic," and it's one of the main reasons privacy-conscious wallets try to
avoid unnecessarily combining unrelated UTXOs, and why techniques like
CoinJoin exist specifically to break this kind of on-chain link.

As in Lab 06, the fee here is simply whatever is left over after subtracting
all outputs from all inputs: 1.2 BTC went in, and 1.19997240 BTC came back out
across the payment and change outputs combined, leaving 0.0000276 BTC as the
fee — confirming that value conservation holds even across a transaction with
multiple inputs, not just the single-input case demonstrated earlier.
